🛰 ADCS SIMULATOR

Simulate a tumbling satellite under an arbitrary-axis spin, then trigger a three-axis reaction wheel correction. Configure the satellite and wheel properties, watch the attitude respond in 3D, and track wheel speed and saturation over time.

How This Simulator Works

This tool models a satellite as a rigid rectangular box to model the different moments of inertia per plane. Two different simulation regimes are available, switched with the Seconds / Adaptive pill above the inputs, and the right-hand side of the page changes shape depending on which one is selected.

Seconds mode

What it models: three phases, which are played back in real time (one second of playback equals one simulated second). Before the button is clicked the satellite tumbles around a constant axis according to the angular velocity inputted, this is obviously unrealistic as it does not model torque free precession, but is simply a way to visualise the initial angular velocity inputted. When the button is clicked the phases begin. Firstly, the satellite tumbles freely under whatever initial angular velocity you set, with no correction applied yet, however this is realistic tumbling (torque free precession) as it uses euler equations for rigid bodies. Second, a proportional-derivative (PD) controller commands the reaction wheels to cancel out both the orientation error and the angular velocity, visibly correcting the tumble. Third, the satellite holds its corrected attitude.

How the inputs affect it:

  • Initial Angular Velocity (X/Y/Z, rad/s): the starting spin rate about each body axis. A value on a single axis produces simple spin; a combination across two or more axes produces the more complex, chaotic-looking precession this simulator is built to demonstrate and correct.
  • Satellite Properties (mass, dimensions): sets the moment of inertia per axis from the standard rectangular-prism formula. Different dimensions per axis give different moments of inertia, which is what makes multi-axis tumbling interesting, via Euler's rigid body equations, rather than each axis behaving independently.
  • Reaction Wheels (mass, radius, max RPM, max spin-up rate): one shared specification applied identically to all three orthogonal wheels. Wheel mass and radius set the wheel's own moment of inertia, which determines how much RPM change is needed to produce a given correction torque. Max RPM is the wheel's momentum storage ceiling: once reached, that axis is saturated and can no longer supply correction torque. Max spin-up rate caps how quickly the wheel's speed, and therefore the torque it can deliver, can change.

What you'll see: the satellite animates in 3D through its full trajectory, the three RPM graphs show each wheel's speed over time, and a status label reports which phase is active (red "Torque-Free Precession", yellow "Applying Correction", green "Stability Achieved", or red "Saturated" if a wheel maxed out before the correction finished).

Limitations: the correction is capped at 100 simulated seconds, if the controller hasn't converged by then, the run ends anyway. Disturbance torque is not modelled in this timeframe at all, it is built as a fast, isolated tumble-and-correct demonstration.

Adaptive mode

What it models: the opposite regime. The satellite starts perfectly stable (any initial tumble is assumed to have already been corrected too quickly to be worth showing at this timescale), and a constant external disturbance torque acts on it continuously. The reaction wheels absorb this torque by building up momentum until they saturate, one axis at a time, and once an axis saturates it can no longer counter the disturbance on that axis.

How the inputs affect it:

  • Reaction Wheels: the same shared specification as seconds mode. Here it directly sets how much momentum each wheel can absorb before saturating (mass, radius, max RPM); a larger wheel or higher max RPM takes longer to saturate under the same torque.
  • Disturbance Torque (magnitude and direction): the constant torque vector. Magnitude is entered in scientific notation (a mantissa from 0 to 10 and a power-of-ten exponent), since realistic disturbance torques span many orders of magnitude. The direction vector's relative component sizes determine how the torque splits across the three axes: an axis with a larger component saturates faster than one with a smaller component, even under the same overall magnitude.

What you'll see: three RPM graphs, one per wheel, each ramping from 0 toward its max RPM (in whichever direction that axis's torque points) until it saturates and flattens out. Saturation and stability pills report each axis's current state (grey means not yet saturated, orange means saturated) and overall stability (green "Satellite Stable" while all three wheels are still countering the disturbance, red "Satellite Unstable" the moment any one of them saturates). A timescale pill reports the real-world time one second of playback represents, since saturation can genuinely take anywhere from seconds to months of real time depending on the inputs, so the whole run is always compressed to play back over a fixed window regardless of how long it actually took.

Limitations: 3D orientation is not modelled in this mode at all. Once a wheel saturates, the resulting tumble happens far too fast according to the newly defined timescale, as potentially hundreds of thousands of rotations can occur before the run ends, so it is left out rather than shown incorrectly. Satellite mass and dimensions have no effect on this mode's output, since without orientation tracking there is no moment-of-inertia-dependent dynamics left to compute here, only the reaction wheels and the disturbance torque matter.

Timeframe

Seconds models a live tumble and the reaction wheel correction in real time. Adaptive assumes the satellite starts stable and applies a constant disturbance torque, rescaled to a fixed playback window since reaching saturation can take anywhere from seconds to months.
